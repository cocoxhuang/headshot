p = 3: the gap is a theorem

Davenport and Heilbronn proved that the mean of |Cl(D)[3]| tends to 2 (imaginary) and 4/3 (real), as Cohen–Lenstra predict. Bhargava–Shankar–Tsimerman and Taniguchi–Thorne found the next term:

Σ0 < ±D < X |Cl(D)[3]| = M±X + K±X5/6 + o(X5/6),   K± = c± · 8ζ(1/3) / (5Γ(2/3)3) · Πp(1 − (p1/3+1)/(p(p+1)))

with M− = 6/π², M+ = 4/π², c− = √3, c+ = 1. Since ζ(1/3) < 0, the correction is negative: K− = −0.4148 and K+ = −0.2395. The mean therefore sits about 1.36·X−1/6 below 2 for imaginary fields. A factor of 10 in X closes only a third of the gap.

Local conditions change the speed, not the limit

Restrict to fields where p splits, stays inert or ramifies in Q(√D). Cohen–Lenstra predict the same limit in each case, and for 3-torsion this is a theorem (Bhargava–Varma). The secondary terms differ, so the subfamilies sit at different distances from the limit.

p = 2: compare at finite rank

Genus theory fixes the 2-rank of Cl(D) at ω(D) − 1, so Cohen–Lenstra does not apply to the 2-part itself. Gerth's version applies to the squares 2·Cl(D); it is proven for 4-ranks (Fouvry–Klüners) and for the full 2-part of imaginary fields (Smith). Because ω(D) grows like log log X, most fields here have 2-rank 1 to 3, far from the t → ∞ limit. The fair comparison is Gerth's law at the observed 2-rank t (corank of a random t×t matrix over F2), averaged over the data.

4-rank of Cl(D), imaginary fields

The finite-t prediction closes most of the gap to the data. The rest comes from slow convergence at fixed t: among fields with 2-rank 2 and |D| < 108, 44.9% have 4-rank 0, against the predicted 37.5%.

Real fields: class number one

Share of real quadratic fields with trivial odd class group

For prime discriminants D = p the class number is odd, and Cohen–Lenstra predict h(p) = 1 with probability Πq odd Πk≥2(1 − q−k) = 75.446%.

What to keep in mind

  1. Fields, signs and ordering. The heuristics are about maximal orders (fundamental discriminants), ordered by |D|. Imaginary fields have unit rank u = 0 and real fields u = 1, which changes every prediction: P(3 | h) is 43.99% versus 15.98%.
  2. p = 2 needs Gerth, at finite rank. Compare 2·Cl(D) with the prediction at the observed 2-rank, not the limit. For real fields the theorem concerns the narrow class group, and these tables hold the ordinary one.
  3. Gaps are systematic. With 3·107 to 3·108 fields, sampling error is about 10−4. Percent-level gaps are secondary terms, and for p = 3 they are proven (X−1/6).
  4. Small groups converge first. h(D) ≈ √|D|·L(1,χ)/π, so pk | h needs h ≥ pk. Real fields are much worse: the regulator absorbs most of h·R. Trust a statistic only when its groups are much smaller than typical class numbers.
  5. Cumulative versus windowed. Frequencies over |D| < X mix all scales below X. Windows near X converge faster but are noisier. Both are in results/*_cumulative.csv.
  6. Moments versus probabilities. The theorems are about moments, E[#Sur(Clp, A)] = |A|−u. Higher moments are driven by rare large groups and converge more slowly: E|Cl[3]|² is 5.49 at 108, against 6.
  7. Independence and local conditions. Test independence of different primes against the product of the observed marginals. Main terms should not depend on local conditions (proven for 3-torsion by Bhargava–Varma), but secondary terms do, so subfamilies converge at different rates.
  8. Hypotheses. Imaginary data is unconditional and was cross-checked against PARI. Real data relies on GRH via PARI's quadclassunit.
